## 3. My Assessment Philosophy

A response should not receive a high score simply because it sounds confident, polished, or comprehensive.

A strong response must satisfy the task, provide reliable information, communicate clearly, maintain logical consistency, and deliver practical value to its intended audience.

I therefore distinguish between surface-level writing quality and substantive response quality.

For example, a response may be grammatically excellent but factually incorrect. Another may contain accurate information but fail to answer the question directly. Both situations require careful assessment rather than an automatic high score.

My goal is to make evaluation decisions objective, consistent, transparent, and supported by evidence from the response itself.

## 4. Assessment Workflow

My proposed workflow for evaluating written responses follows seven stages.

### Stage 1: Understand the instructions

I identify:

- The main task and intended outcome.
- The target audience.
- Required information.
- Formatting and length constraints.
- Any explicit restrictions or special instructions.

### Stage 2: Review responses independently

I read each response carefully before comparing alternatives. This helps reduce the risk of judging one response primarily in relation to another rather than against the actual requirements.

### Stage 3: Apply the rubric consistently

I evaluate each response against the same assessment dimensions and scoring definitions.

Where a project provides its own rubric, I follow that rubric rather than substituting personal preferences or unrelated criteria.

### Stage 4: Record evidence

I identify specific statements, omissions, contradictions, or wording problems that support each important score.

I avoid vague explanations such as "the answer is poor" when I can explain exactly what is incorrect, incomplete, confusing, or unsupported.

### Stage 5: Compare response quality

I consider which response best satisfies the task, supports its claims, explains its reasoning, and serves the intended audience.

I do not automatically reward a longer response over a concise one.

### Stage 6: Conduct a quality check

I review the scores and written rationales to ensure that the judgments are consistent, relevant, and supported by observable evidence.

### Stage 7: Document and escalate

I record the final assessment and flag unclear instructions, ambiguous rubric definitions, or unresolved disagreements for clarification.

## 5. Sample Assessment Rubric

The following is an example rubric developed for this portfolio. Each dimension is scored from 1 to 5.

| Dimension | 1 — Poor | 3 — Adequate | 5 — Excellent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instruction Adherence | Misses the main task or violates key requirements. | Addresses the main task but misses a requirement. | Fully satisfies the instructions and constraints. |
| Accuracy | Contains major factual errors or unsupported claims. | Mostly accurate but includes a material gap or qualification issue. | Accurate, appropriately qualified, and well supported. |
| Clarity and Tone | Difficult to understand or unsuitable for the audience. | Understandable but uneven or unnecessarily wordy. | Clear, coherent, concise, and audience-appropriate. |
| Reasoning and Consistency | Contains serious contradictions or logical errors. | Generally understandable but has some reasoning gaps. | Coherent, relevant, and internally consistent. |
| Practical Usefulness | Fails to solve the problem or could mislead the reader. | Partly useful but incomplete. | Directly addresses the need and provides usable information. |

### Scoring principles

1. Each dimension is assessed independently.
2. Scores must reflect the project's scoring definitions.
3. Important weaknesses should be supported by specific evidence.
4. A response should not receive a high accuracy score simply because it sounds authoritative.
5. Personal preference should not replace the stated requirements.
6. If the project supplies weighted criteria, those weights must be applied as instructed.
7. When evidence is insufficient to support a judgment, the limitation should be documented.

## 6. Practical Demonstration: Comparing Two AI-Generated Responses

### Evaluation prompt

"Explain photosynthesis to a 12-year-old in three to four sentences. Include why sunlight matters."

### Response A

Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They use energy from sunlight to turn water and carbon dioxide into sugar, releasing oxygen as a by-product. Sunlight provides the energy needed for this process. When a plant receives insufficient light, its rate of photosynthesis may decrease.

### Response B

Photosynthesis is how plants eat soil. They take oxygen from the air and turn it into sunlight, which becomes food. Sunlight is mainly useful because it warms the leaves. Every plant can photosynthesize at the same rate regardless of how much light it receives.

### Comparative Assessment

| Assessment Dimension | Response A | Response B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Instruction Adherence | 5/5 | 4/5 |
| Accuracy | 5/5 | 1/5 |
| Clarity and Tone | 5/5 | 3/5 |
| Reasoning and Consistency | 5/5 | 1/5 |
| Practical Usefulness | 5/5 | 1/5 |
| **Average Score** | **5.0/5** | **2.0/5** |

These scores are illustrative judgments under the sample rubric above.

### Rationale: Instruction Adherence

Both responses contain four sentences and address sunlight. Response A satisfies the task while providing a suitable explanation for the intended audience.

Response B follows the requested format, but its scientific inaccuracies undermine its ability to fulfil the educational purpose.

### Rationale: Accuracy

Response A correctly identifies water and carbon dioxide as inputs and explains sunlight's role as an energy source.

Response B contains major scientific errors. It incorrectly describes plants as turning oxygen into sunlight and makes a false generalization about the effect of light availability on photosynthesis.

These errors affect the central learning objective and justify a very low accuracy score.

### Rationale: Clarity and Tone

Response A uses direct, accessible language appropriate for a young learner.

Response B is readable at the sentence level, but readable language does not make incorrect information acceptable. Its confident presentation could make the misconceptions more persuasive.

### Rationale: Reasoning and Consistency

Response A maintains a coherent explanation of the process.

Response B confuses the roles of sunlight and oxygen and presents an unjustified claim that light availability makes no difference to photosynthesis.

### Rationale: Practical Usefulness

Response A provides a useful basic explanation that a young learner can understand.

Response B could teach incorrect concepts and should not be used as an educational explanation without substantial correction.

### Overall Decision

Response A is substantially stronger because it combines instruction adherence, accuracy, clarity, coherent reasoning, and educational usefulness.

Response B demonstrates why evaluation must go beyond fluency. Its language is understandable, but its factual errors make it unsuitable for the intended purpose.

The most important revision is to correct the scientific explanation before making further stylistic improvements.

## 7. Example of a Professional Assessment Rationale

**Assessment decision: Response B requires substantial revision.**

Response B follows the requested sentence limit and uses accessible language, but its central explanation contains significant scientific inaccuracies. In particular, it incorrectly states that plants turn oxygen into sunlight and that photosynthesis occurs at the same rate regardless of light availability. These errors undermine the educational objective and could leave the intended reader with misconceptions. I would prioritize correcting the scientific content, then review the revised explanation for clarity, logical consistency, and age appropriateness.

### Why this rationale is effective

- It identifies the strengths of the response without overstating them.
- It cites specific factual errors.
- It explains why the errors matter to the intended audience.
- It prioritizes the most important corrective action.
- It distinguishes factual accuracy from writing style.

## 8. Handling Reviewer Disagreement

When two reviewers disagree materially about a score, I would investigate the source of the disagreement before deciding on a final assessment.

My process would be:

1. Identify the specific assessment dimension and passage responsible for the disagreement.
2. Compare each reviewer's reasoning with the prompt and the applicable rubric definition.
3. Check whether a reviewer overlooked evidence, interpreted an instruction differently, or applied an inconsistent standard.
4. Determine whether the disagreement reflects an individual scoring error, an ambiguous rubric, or a need for reviewer calibration.
5. Document the evidence supporting the final decision.
6. Escalate unresolved ambiguity to the project lead.
7. Apply any clarified guidance consistently to subsequent evaluations.

I would not automatically average conflicting scores without investigating why they differ.

The objective is to reach a defensible decision while improving consistency across future assessments.

## 9. Quality-Control Checklist

Before submitting an assessment, I would verify the following:

### Instruction Adherence
- Have all required instructions been considered?
- Does the response satisfy the requested format and audience requirements?
- Are any important elements missing?

### Accuracy
- Are factual statements reliable?
- Are claims supported by appropriate evidence?
- Are uncertainty and limitations represented honestly?

### Clarity and Tone
- Is the response understandable?
- Is the tone appropriate for the intended audience?
- Is unnecessary repetition affecting readability?

### Reasoning and Consistency
- Does the argument follow logically?
- Are there contradictions or unsupported conclusions?
- Do the conclusions follow from the information provided?

### Scoring and Documentation
- Does each score match the rubric definition?
- Is the rationale supported by specific evidence?
- Have the same standards been applied to all responses?
- Are unresolved issues documented appropriately?

## 11. Applying These Skills to AI Evaluation

When evaluating AI-generated writing, I would focus on whether each response meets the actual requirements rather than simply whether it sounds polished.

I would assess:

- Whether the response follows the prompt.
- Whether its factual claims are accurate and appropriately qualified.
- Whether its reasoning is coherent.
- Whether its tone suits the intended audience.
- Whether it provides useful and relevant information.
- Whether its weaknesses are clearly documented.
- Whether its score is justified by the applicable rubric.

I would also remain open to project-specific guidance, calibration exercises, and revised assessment criteria.

I understand that AI evaluation requires consistent application of instructions and rubrics. My goal would be to produce judgments that another reviewer can understand, verify, and apply consistently.
